Generating lipids, not molecules

A model can draw a plausible ionizable lipid in seconds. The hard part is proposing one that can be made, formulated, and tested in the setting where it has to work.

When people talk about generative design for lipid nanoparticles, they usually show a chemical structure. That picture makes the problem look cleaner than it is.

The molecule isn't the unit

An ionizable lipid is a molecule, of course. But it only becomes useful after it is mixed with a helper phospholipid, cholesterol, a PEG-lipid, and nucleic-acid cargo. Together they assemble into a particle. Change the molar ratios, the buffer, or the mixing conditions and the same lipid can give you a different particle with different size, encapsulation, stability, and activity. So although the model outputs a molecular structure, the thing we care about is that structure in a formulation, in an assay, at a particular dose.

This distinction matters because most machine learning work on LNPs begins with a closed library. A model ranks the lipids in that library and an experimentalist tests the promising ones. It is a useful way to spend fewer experiments, but the chemistry has already been chosen. A generative model takes on a harder job: it has to propose a lipid that was not in the library and give us a reason to make it.

Start with the chemistry

Ionizable lipids have a recognizable anatomy. The headgroup controls protonation. The linkers affect degradation and clearance. The length, branching, and saturation of the tails change how the lipid packs into a particle. These features interact, so there is no simple lookup table from a structural change to a biological outcome, but they are still useful structure for a model. Treating the lipid as a generic graph ignores information a chemist would use from the beginning.

The first practical question is whether the proposed lipid can be made. A valid SMILES string does not answer it. Nor does a favorable synthetic-accessibility score. In a real campaign, the available building blocks and reactions define the part of chemical space we can reach on a useful timescale. If a generator ignores those constraints, the chemist becomes a filter for its output.

I would rather put the chemistry into the generation process. A model can work with building blocks, preserve the core of a known reaction, or generate a synthetic route along with the product. The exact representation matters less than the guarantee it provides: when a candidate scores well, we should already know how we intend to make it. That is the idea behind our work on synthesis-constrained diffusion. We hold the reaction-defined part of the lipid fixed and generate only the parts the chemistry allows us to vary.

The label belongs to the experiment

The next problem is the data. A transfection measurement is often treated as a label attached to a lipid structure. It is really a label attached to an experiment. The result also depends on the formulation, cargo, dose, route of administration, cell or animal model, and assay time point. Leave those fields out and two measurements that look comparable may describe different things.

This is especially awkward in the public literature. Successful lipids are much easier to find than failed ones, and formulation details are reported unevenly. Closely related analogues also tend to appear in the same series. If those analogues are split randomly between training and test sets, a model can look accurate by recognizing a chemical family it has already seen. A scaffold or series split is less flattering, but it gives a better answer to the question we actually care about: will the model help with new chemistry?

For our purposes, a useful record starts with the lipid and its synthetic route, then keeps the formulation and assay metadata beside the result. Particle measurements such as size, polydispersity, and encapsulation efficiency belong there too. This is tedious experimental bookkeeping. It is also what turns a collection of potency values into data a generative system can learn from.

A predictor is not the objective

Once a model can generate makeable lipids, it still needs some way to choose among them. Potency is the obvious score, and on its own it is a bad target. A lipid can transfect well and still be unstable, difficult to formulate, poorly tolerated, or active in the wrong tissue. The trade-offs are biological: membrane disruption can help endosomal escape while also contributing to toxicity.

A search procedure will also find weaknesses in a predictor. Push hard enough on a model trained from a small, narrow dataset and it will eventually recommend structures that receive high predicted scores because they are far from anything the model understands. Uncertainty helps, but it does not replace experiments. I trust a workflow more when it uses several readouts, states which ones guided the search, and reserves genuinely independent tests for the end.

Formulation cannot be deferred to that final test. At minimum, the scoring model should know the co-lipids, ratios, cargo, and processing conditions used to produce a measurement. With enough data, lipid and formulation could be designed together. We are not there yet. For now, even treating formulation as part of the input would be an improvement over assigning each lipid a context-free potency.

The benchmark ends at the bench

Chemical validity and novelty are useful diagnostics, but they are not evidence that a generator has found a better lipid. The meaningful accounting happens after generation: how many candidates had credible routes, how many were made and purified, how many formed acceptable particles, and how many performed well in the intended assay. Reporting the losses at each step is more informative than reporting a large number of generated structures.

The strongest test is prospective. Let the model choose candidates, make them, and report the results, including the failures. Show how similar each candidate was to the training set and keep related chemical series out of the test data. That makes it possible to tell the difference between a system that explores and one that produces small variations on familiar lipids.

In the near term, I am not trying to have one model design an entire nanoparticle. The narrower goal is already difficult: propose new ionizable lipids that fit chemistry we can run, then judge them in the formulation and biological setting we care about. If that loop works reliably, co-designing the rest of the particle becomes a reasonable next problem. Drawing more molecules is not the bottleneck.
